Can AI Help Me Save a 30-Year-Old Christmas Tradition?

Six weeks, one family tradition, and an experiment in building something that brings people together.

By Jenna Clark · November 4, 2026
The experiment
Family & technology
Six-week build sprint

Project overview ↗

Every Christmas Day after dinner, my mother-in-law brings out her famous Argos game.

For years, she's painstakingly cut pictures from the iconic Argos catalog, blacked out the prices with a Sharpie, and assembled them into a family guessing game. We pass the sheets around, scribble down our guesses, and argue over who's already had which sheet.

Closest to the actual price wins bragging rights and, of course, scratch-off lottery tickets. Who doesn't love a little gambling on Christmas?

In 2020, Argos announced it was retiring its printed catalog after 47 years and more than a billion copies. Thankfully, my mother-in-law had hoarded a backup stash. Crisis averted.

But keeping the tradition going has required some creativity.

During the pandemic, she mailed the game sheets to us in New York so we could play together over video. We're now in Seattle, my brother-in-law is in Portugal, and the rest of the family is in the UK.

Three countries. Two time zones. One very determined mother-in-law.

The Game Must Go On

We've talked for years about turning the Argos game into an app. Something that would let everyone play together, wherever we happened to be. But between work, life, and the fact that I don't write software for a living, it never quite happened.

Until now.

The idea is simple: a live, interactive game with a shared screen displaying the catalog items and everyone submitting their price guesses from their phones. Same family competition, fewer envelopes crossing the Atlantic.

Here's Where AI Comes In

I've spent years building teams, systems, and enterprise technology, often working alongside engineers to bring an idea to life. But I'm not a software developer.

AI is changing what's possible for people like me who know what they want to build but haven't traditionally written the code themselves.

This is exciting, but it comes with an important caveat, and one I'll be exploring throughout this series.

AI can accelerate development, but it does not eliminate the need for sound architecture, thoughtful testing, or human judgment.

Generating code is one thing. Building something that actually works reliably for real people is another.

So I'm giving myself six weeks to see how far I can take this.

I'll be building a real-time web app, figuring out how to connect phones to a shared screen, experimenting with AI-assisted development, and undoubtedly breaking a few things along the way.

And I'm going to share the process: the tools, the decisions, the mistakes, what works, and what doesn't.

Why Share All of This?

Because I think we need to be more honest about AI.

We need more practical AI literacy and more transparency about how these tools are actually being used.

It's easy to show a polished result. It's far more useful to understand what went into making it, which tools helped, where they fell short, and where human judgment made the difference.

Every entry in this series will include AI Receipts: a transparent account of the tools I use, how I use them, what works, and what I learn along the way.

This isn't another series about AI buzzwords. It's an actual project with a real deadline and a family waiting to play it on Christmas Day.

Hopefully, by the end of it, we'll have more than a working game. We'll have learned something useful about what AI makes possible, where it falls short, and how to use it thoughtfully.

And if all goes according to plan, we'll have found a new way to keep an old tradition alive, even when we're thousands of miles apart.

Six weeks. One family tradition. The game must go on.

This is a personal, independent project inspired by a family game using historic Argos catalogs. It is not affiliated with or endorsed by Argos. The game is in development; no public playable version is promised yet.
